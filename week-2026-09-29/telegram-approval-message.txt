LinkedIn caption:
Not every service failure has the same weight.

That is why I like the Zone of Tolerance as a CX framework. It forces leaders to stop treating every metric as equal.

A guest may tolerate a short wait if the team communicates clearly.
They may tolerate a missing item if recovery is fast and accountable.
They may not tolerate uncertainty, indifference, or a handoff where nobody owns the answer.

The executive question is not "Did we meet the standard?"

It is sharper than that:

Where does the guest move from patient to disappointed?

That threshold is where mystery shopping becomes useful as an instrument. It gives leadership evidence on which failures matter most, which standards need governance, and where the prescription should focus.

Average scores are comfortable. Thresholds are operational.

And in CX, the threshold is where the decision is made.

2. Thursday post - Field File: The Handoff That Lost The Visit
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/captainredbaron/social_media/main/week-2026-09-29/thursday-field-file-handoff-lost-visit.jpg

Caption:
Field File: The Handoff That Lost The Visit.

I reviewed an anonymized mystery shop recently where the issue was not the first answer.

It was the handoff.

The guest asked a reasonable question. The first team member did not know the answer, which can happen. The problem came next: the guest was sent to a second colleague, then a third, with no ownership, no explanation, and no clear close.

By the end, the guest had not received a terrible answer.

They had received no answer.

That matters because customers often tolerate delay when they can see accountability. What they do not tolerate is being passed around without a visible owner.

For leadership, the recommendation is simple:

Measure handoffs as a service behaviour, not an operational footnote.

A good handoff has three parts:
Ownership. Context. Closure.

Without those, the customer is not moving through a service journey. They are being transferred through uncertainty.
