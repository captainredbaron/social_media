Caption:
In customer experience, the decisive moments are often smaller than executives expect.

A greeting that makes the guest feel recognized.
A delay explained before frustration hardens.
A handoff where the next employee already knows the context.
A recovery where ownership is clear.

Jan Carlzon called these moments of truth: the points where the customer forms an impression of the entire company.

That idea still matters because customers do not grade our departments separately. They remember the moments where the brand either becomes credible or becomes theatre.

This is why I like mystery shopping as an instrument. It does not only tell us whether the standard exists. It shows whether the standard survived contact with the customer.

If a team knows its moments of truth, training becomes sharper. Measurement becomes cleaner. Governance becomes easier to defend.

If it does not, the business ends up managing averages while customers remember exceptions.

What is the one moment of truth your customer journey cannot afford to mishandle?

## Thursday, August 13 - Field File
Topic: Silence Before Frustration
Image: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/captainredbaron/social_media/main/week-2026-08-11/thursday-field-file-silence-before-frustration.jpg

Caption:
Field File: the moment before frustration.

In one anonymized visit, the guest waited longer than expected.

That was not the real issue.

The real issue was silence.

For several minutes, nobody explained the delay, reset the expectation, or took ownership of the next step. When the employee finally returned, the answer was technically correct. But the moment of truth had already passed.

This is where many service experiences lose trust. Not in the delay itself, but in the unmanaged space around it.

A customer can accept waiting when the business shows control. Silence makes the customer wonder whether anyone is in control at all.

In my view, mystery shopping is valuable because it captures that exact point: the moment before frustration becomes the customer's final memory.

Where does your team need to speak earlier, before the customer fills the silence themselves?
