Caption:
I have never believed that service quality lives only in the smile, the greeting, or the final thank-you.

Those moments matter. But they are usually the visible edge of a much larger system.

In 1984, G. Lynn Shostack introduced Service Blueprinting: a way to map what the customer sees against the backstage actions that make the experience possible.

That idea is still underused.

When a guest repeats the same request twice, the problem may not be attitude. It may be handover.

When a premium customer is not recognized, the problem may not be training. It may be data flow.

When a recovery feels slow, the problem may not be care. It may be decision authority.

This is why mystery shopping should not be treated as a scorecard of frontline behavior alone.

Done properly, it becomes evidence of the system behind the experience.

Where do you think most service breakdowns really begin: frontstage or backstage?

## Thursday, July 9 - Field File
Topic: The Invisible Handoff
Image: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/captainredbaron/social_media/main/week-2026-07-07/thursday-field-file-invisible-handoff.jpg

Caption:
Field File: the handoff that changed the visit.

In one anonymized service visit, the guest made a simple request at arrival:

"Please let housekeeping know I need the room ready before my afternoon meeting."

The first employee noted it.
The second employee never saw it.
The third employee apologized.

Nobody was careless. The handoff was simply invisible.

That is the dangerous part of CX: the guest experiences one brand, but the operation is often several teams passing small details between each other.

When the note finally reached the right person, the recovery was strong.

But the evidence was already there.

The failure was not the delay. The failure was that the system had no reliable way to protect the guest's original request.

In my view, if a guest has to repeat a preference, the brand has already spent some of its trust.

What is the most common handoff risk in your customer journey?
